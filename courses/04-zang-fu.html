The body according to the Neijing: zang-fu 脏腑

The organ names are the single most misunderstood part of Chinese medicine, and the misunderstanding is mostly a translation problem. The Neijing's organs are offices, not anatomy. Once you see that, the system's coherence and its limits both become obvious in the same moment - and two famous examples stop being embarrassing and start being interesting.

Part 0

Why this session exists

Almost every argument about Chinese medicine on the internet is really an argument about words. Someone says the TCM liver is not the liver; someone else says of course it is, it is right there in the name. Both are talking past each other, because 肝 and "liver" are not the same kind of noun. This session's thesis is one sentence: the Neijing's organs are offices, not anatomy. They are defined by what they do in a system, the way "the treasury" names a function rather than a building. Hold that, and the system's internal coherence and its hard limits arrive together, in the same breath.

Part 1 · covers the founding distinction

Store versus transmit 7 min live

素问 chapter 11, 五藏别论 - "A Separate Discourse on the Five Zang" - draws the line that organizes the whole organ system. It is not a line between chest and belly, or between solid and hollow. It is a line between two jobs.

✦ 素问 · 五藏别论 (Suwen ch.11)所謂五藏者，藏精氣而不瀉也，故滿而不能實。六府者，傳化物而不藏，故實而不能滿也。 Suǒwèi wǔ zàng zhě, cáng jīng qì ér bù xiè yě, gù mǎn ér bù néng shí. Liù fǔ zhě, chuán huà wù ér bù cáng, gù shí ér bù néng mǎn yě. "The five zang store essence-qi and do not discharge it; therefore they can be full but not stuffed. The six fu transmit and transform substances but do not store; therefore they can be stuffed but not full."
LiveWhat the distinction actually says3.5 min▶

The text says (Suwen ch.11). Two categories, defined entirely by behaviour.

  • 五脏 the five zang - 肝 liver, 心 heart, 脾 spleen, 肺 lung, 肾 kidney. They store 精氣 jīng qì, essence-qi, and do not discharge it. Hence 滿而不能實: they can be full but not stuffed.
  • 六腑 the six fu - 胆 gallbladder, 胃 stomach, 大肠 large intestine, 小肠 small intestine, 三焦 triple burner, 膀胱 bladder. They transmit and transform substances and do not store. Hence 實而不能滿: they can be stuffed but not full.

The 滿/實 wordplay is doing precise work. 滿 mǎn is the fullness of a reservoir at its proper level - a steady state. 實 shí is the fullness of a pipe with something in it right now - a transient. A reservoir that becomes a blocked pipe is malfunctioning, and so is a pipe that starts behaving like a reservoir.

Say it in one line and the whole system reorganizes around it: zang are reservoirs, fu are conduits. That is a claim about storage versus throughput. It is not a claim about which organs are solid and which are hollow, and it certainly is not a claim derived from opening a body and looking.

Real world

Why this matters for reading anything else in the canon. Once organs are defined by job, an "organ" can exist in the system without existing on a dissection table - it only has to have a job. That is not a loophole the tradition hides; it is a direct consequence of the definition, and Part 3 shows the tradition being completely candid about the most famous case.

Self-study奇恒之腑 - the extraordinary fu, and a double-booked organ1.5 min read▶

The same chapter names a third category that sits awkwardly across the line: 奇恒之腑 qí héng zhī fǔ, the "extraordinary fu" - 脑 brain, 髓 marrow, 骨 bone, 脉 vessels, 胆 gallbladder, 女子胞 uterus.

Their defining feature is that they are shaped like fu but behave like zang: hollow in form, storing in function. The neat two-way sort turns out to need an exceptions bin, which is what usually happens to neat two-way sorts.

Note the 胆 gallbladder in that list. It is double-listed - counted among the six ordinary fu and among the extraordinary ones. The tradition notices and discusses it rather than quietly dropping one entry, which is the same intellectual habit you saw with 长夏 in Session 3: the seam is visible in the primary text to anyone who reads carefully.

Part 2 · covers the court metaphor

Twelve officials 8 min live

素问 chapter 8, 灵兰秘典论, is the clearest proof in the entire canon that these organs are functions. It does not describe tissue. It hands out government posts, and then says what issues from each one.

✦ 素问 · 灵兰秘典论 (Suwen ch.8)心者，君主之官也，神明出焉。肺者，相傅之官，治節出焉。肝者，將軍之官，謀慮出焉。膽者，中正之官，決斷出焉。脾胃者，倉廩之官，五味出焉。三焦者，決瀆之官，水道出焉。膀胱者，州都之官，津液藏焉，氣化則能出矣。 Xīn zhě, jūnzhǔ zhī guān yě, shénmíng chū yān. Fèi zhě, xiàngfù zhī guān, zhìjié chū yān. Gān zhě, jiāngjūn zhī guān, móulǜ chū yān. Dǎn zhě, zhōngzhèng zhī guān, juéduàn chū yān. Pí wèi zhě, cānglǐn zhī guān, wǔwèi chū yān. Sānjiāo zhě, juédú zhī guān, shuǐdào chū yān. Pángguāng zhě, zhōudū zhī guān, jīnyè cáng yān, qìhuà zé néng chū yǐ. "The heart is the office of the sovereign; spirit brightness issues from it. The lung is the office of the chancellor; regulation issues from it. The liver is the office of the general; planning and deliberation issue from it. The gallbladder is the office of the upright judge; decision issues from it. The spleen and stomach are the office of the granary; the five flavours issue from them. The triple burner is the office of the clearer of dykes; the waterways issue from it. The bladder is the office of the regional capital; fluids are stored there, and when qi transforms them they can be issued."

The text says (Suwen ch.8). Read the third column and notice that every entry is an output - something the office produces or releases. That is a job description.

The chapter is a sustained political metaphor doing medical work. A general plans; a chancellor keeps the rhythm of administration; a judge decides; a granary handles supply; a hydraulic engineer clears the dykes. The body is being described as a well-run state, which is exactly what correlative cosmology promised in Session 2: the cosmos, the state and the body get sorted with one vocabulary.

A textual note worth having Some editions read 食廪之官 rather than 倉廩之官 for the spleen and stomach. This course uses 倉廩, "the granary" - but you will meet the variant, and knowing that a character can differ between editions is the Session 1 lesson arriving in practice.
LiveWhat each zang stores - Suwen ch.232 min▶

The text says (素问 ch.23 宣明五气). Five clauses, one per zang, assigning each a mental or spiritual charge:

✦ 素问 · 宣明五气 (Suwen ch.23)心藏神，肺藏魄，肝藏魂，脾藏意，肾藏志。 Xīn cáng shén, fèi cáng pò, gān cáng hún, pí cáng yì, shèn cáng zhì. "The heart stores shen (spirit), the lung stores po (the corporeal soul), the liver stores hun (the ethereal soul), the spleen stores yi (thought, intention), the kidney stores zhi (will, purpose)."

Set this next to ch.11 and the design becomes visible. Chapter 11 said the zang store and do not discharge. Chapter 23 says what each one stores. The two chapters are one idea in two instalments, and 神 shén appears in both this list and the ch.8 office of the heart - the sovereign's office is also the storehouse of spirit.

Do not over-translate 魂 hún and 魄 pò. English "soul" carries freight this vocabulary does not; the safest handling is to keep the Chinese term, give the standard gloss, and leave it there.

Part 3 · covers the two hard cases

Two honest examples 8 min live

These are the cases every critic reaches for and every defender fumbles. Handled precisely, they are the best teaching material in the course - one is a translation problem, the other is a system being candid about its own arithmetic.

LiveExample 1 · the spleen, a translation artefact4 min▶

The standard debunk goes: the ancients thought the spleen digested food, and they were wrong, because the spleen is an immune and blood-filtering organ. That sentence is tidy, popular, and the wrong analysis.

Here is the accurate version. TCM 脾 pí is a functionally defined system - digestion, absorption, distribution of what food yields. The anatomical spleen is an immune and haematological organ. The two overlap in essentially nothing. But that is not a factual error the Neijing made; it is what happens when a functional term gets handed an English organ name.

  • The text says: 脾胃者，倉廩之官，五味出焉 - the spleen and stomach are the granary office, from which the five flavours issue (Suwen ch.8). A supply-and-storage post. Nothing about an anatomical structure.
  • Tradition holds: 主运化 zhǔ yùn huà, "governs transformation and transportation," is the later systematized textbook formulation built on that office. It is a fine summary. It is not a Neijing sentence, and it should never be quoted as one.

So the honest framing is this: translating 脾 with the English word "spleen" is a translation artefact that creates a false comparison. You are not comparing two theories of one organ. You are comparing an office with a piece of tissue, because a dictionary put them under the same headword four hundred years ago. Once you say "the granary office" instead, the supposed error evaporates - and so does any temptation to treat the office as a physiological claim about the actual spleen.

Real world

Where this bites in practice. A page says "TCM strengthens the spleen." A reader pictures the organ under their left ribs and reasonably asks what that means for someone who has had a splenectomy. The question is unanswerable because the sentence has silently swapped an office for a tissue. Your homework is to go find one of these in the wild - they take about ninety seconds to locate.

LiveExample 2 · 三焦 sanjiao, the organ with no form4 min▶

This is the best honest example in Chinese medicine, and it is honest because the tradition says it first.

三焦 sānjiāo, usually rendered "triple burner" or "triple energizer," is counted as one of the six fu. It has no anatomical counterpart - no organ, no structure, nothing to point at. The WHO's nomenclature committee kept the untranslated Chinese term for exactly this reason: there is no English organ name to borrow, and borrowing one would repeat the spleen mistake.

  • The text says: the Neijing's contribution is the office. 三焦者，決瀆之官，水道出焉 - the sanjiao is the clearer of dykes, and the waterways issue from it (Suwen ch.8). A job in the water-management system of the body-state.
  • Attribution you must get right: the famous line 有名而无形 yǒu míng ér wú xíng, "it has a name but no form," is from 《难经》 the Nanjing (ch.25 and ch.38), NOT the Neijing. It is quoted as Neijing constantly. It is not.

And the Nanjing poses the whole thing as arithmetic. Chapter 38 asks, in effect: how is it that there are five zang but six fu? The system needs the fu count to come out right against yin-yang symmetry and the five-phase pairings. It gets a sixth fu. And it says, without embarrassment, that the sixth has a name but no form.

Real world

The same move, second instance. In Session 3 you watched five phases meet four seasons and 长夏 late summer appear in Suwen ch.22 to fill the gap. This is that move again: a scheme requires a slot, the slot gets filled, and the tradition writes down what it did. A system that patches itself in public is telling you exactly what kind of system it is - one that fits phenomena into a scheme, rather than deriving a scheme from phenomena. Two clean instances in two sessions is not cherry-picking; it is the method showing itself.

Part 4 · covers the body's materials

What a body is made of 6 min live

灵枢 chapter 30, 决气, does something the canon rarely does: it stops and defines its terms. And it starts with Huangdi admitting he is confused - a reader's question, written into the classic.

✦ 灵枢 · 决气 (Lingshu ch.30) - Huangdi's question余聞人有精、氣、津、液、血、脈，余意以為一氣耳，今乃辨為六名，余不知其所以然。 Yú wén rén yǒu jīng, qì, jīn, yè, xuè, mài, yú yì yǐwéi yī qì ěr, jīn nǎi biàn wéi liù míng, yú bùzhī qí suǒyǐrán. "I have heard people speak of jing, qi, jin, ye, xue and mai. I thought it was all just one qi, yet it is divided into six names, and I do not know why this is so."
LiveThe six, each with the text's own image3.5 min▶

The text says (Lingshu ch.30). Qi Bo answers term by term, and the definitions are pictures rather than measurements.

TermGlossThe text's own image
精 jīngessence"Two spirits join to form a shape" - born before the body itself
氣 qìqiThe upper burner opens and diffuses, spreading the flavours of the five grains, "like the soaking of mist and dew"
津 jīnthe thin fluidSweat-like: the light, freely moving fluid
液 yèthe thick fluidThe heavy fluid of joints, brain and marrow
血 xuèblood"The middle burner takes the juice and transforms it to red"
脈 màithe vessels"Dams and channels the ying qi so it has nowhere to escape"

Read that column and you can see the ontology being built. Every definition is about what a thing does and where it comes from, never about what it would weigh or how it would appear under a lens. This is the same design principle as the offices in ch.8, applied one level down: the body is a set of functions all the way through.

Framed neutrally, which is the only way to frame it: this is the text's own functional ontology. The course neither asserts nor denies that these six terms correspond to anything measurable. It reports what the chapter says and what kind of statement it is.

Self-study津液 as a merged category, and where 神 sits1.5 min read▶

Two footnotes worth carrying, both of which stop later confusion.

津液 is usually treated as one compound category - "body fluids," a single item on most modern lists. This chapter shows that it is a merge of two distinguished terms: 津 the thin and 液 the thick, defined separately, with different distributions. Watching a two-term distinction collapse into one compound word is a small, precise example of how a tradition simplifies itself over two thousand years.

神 shén is not on the list of six. It sits apart, and it gets its placement from the two chapters you met earlier: 心藏神, the heart stores shen (Suwen ch.23), and 神明出焉, spirit brightness issues from the heart (Suwen ch.8). So shen is not a material the body is made of - it is what the sovereign's office produces and holds. Keeping it off the substance list is a distinction the text makes; it is worth not smoothing over.

Add the organ column ✦ 10 min · builds your map

Your map already has five phase columns with a zang in each from Session 3. Now those rows get their jobs - and two of them get an annotation that will save you from the two commonest mistakes in the field.

Give every zang its office. Down the zang row, add the ch.8 post: heart = sovereign, lung = chancellor, liver = general, spleen (with stomach) = granary. Write the 汉字 next to your English. The offices are what the organ names actually mean in this system.

Add what each one stores from ch.23: 神 shen, 魄 po, 魂 hun, 意 yi, 志 zhi. Keep the Chinese terms; the English glosses go in brackets, not the other way round.

Annotate the spleen row in your seam colour: translation artefact - 倉廩之官 is the text; 主运化 is the textbook. This single line is the difference between understanding the spleen case and repeating either side of the usual argument.

Annotate the sanjiao row: no anatomical counterpart; 有名而无形 is the Nanjing, not the Neijing. Put the sanjiao in your fu list even though it has no form - that is precisely the point, and the map should show the system as it is.

Add the six substances in a corner block, and your journal line for today. You are now four sessions in; skim back through the journal and notice whether anything has a shape yet.

Homework

Before Session 5 ◐ 20-25 min total

  • Journal daily - one line, three words. Session 5 is the one that reads your journal back to you against a Han text.
  • Find one online claim about a TCM organ and check the swap. Does the page silently trade the functional organ for the anatomical one, or does it keep them distinct? The tell is usually a sentence that would only make sense about tissue - blood filtration, bile ducts, hepatic function - sitting next to a claim that only makes sense about an office. Write down the sentence where the swap happens.
  • Say the store-versus-transmit line from memory - full but not stuffed, stuffed but not full. It is the whole organ system in eight words.
  • Optional: read Suwen ch.8 on ctext.org. It is short, and the court metaphor is unmistakable even in translation - you will see the officials handed out one after another and recognize the table you just built.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Suwen ch.11 distinguishes the five zang from the six fu by...

"Full but not stuffed" versus "stuffed but not full." Zang are reservoirs, fu are conduits. It is a storage-versus-throughput contrast, not an anatomical one.

2 · Why is "spleen" a translation problem?

脾 is the 倉廩之官, the granary office - a supply function. The anatomical spleen is an immune and haematological organ. They are not two theories of one thing; the shared English word invented the comparison. (主运化 is the later textbook gloss, not the text.)

3 · 有名而无形, "it has a name but no form," comes from...

The Neijing gives the sanjiao its office - 決瀆之官，水道出焉 (ch.8). The candid "name but no form" line is the Nanjing, which frames the sixth fu as an arithmetic requirement. This one is misattributed constantly.
