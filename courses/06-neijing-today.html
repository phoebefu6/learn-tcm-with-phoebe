Part 0

Why this session exists

Almost everything written about Chinese medicine in English falls into one of two failures. The credulous version cites a Han-dynasty chapter as though it settled a clinical question. The sneering version treats two millennia of intellectual work as a single embarrassment. Both are lazy, and both are avoidable with one move: state the stance plainly, then keep the two questions apart. Is the Neijing a serious, sophisticated, historically enormous piece of human thought? Yes, and Sessions 1 through 5 showed you why. Is it a source of medical truth about your body? That is a different question, answered by studies rather than by chapters - and today you get the studies, with their exact numbers.

Part 1 · covers the acupuncture evidence base

Acupuncture: the honest numbers 10 min live

Two sentences you will hear constantly: "acupuncture works" and "acupuncture is placebo." Both are overclaims, and the best evidence says something more interesting than either. The trick is to keep two comparisons apart - acupuncture against fake acupuncture, and acupuncture against nothing at all. They give different answers, and the space between them is the entire argument.

LiveVickers 2012 - the individual patient data meta-analysis4 min▶

Research says. The reference study is Vickers et al 2012, Archives of Internal Medicine 172(19):1444-53 - an individual patient data meta-analysis, which is the strong form: raw patient-level data rather than pooled published summaries. 29 randomised controlled trials, 17,922 patients, chronic pain conditions.

Conditionvs sham acupuncture (SMD)vs no acupuncture (SMD)
Back and neck pain0.23 (95% CI 0.13 to 0.33)0.55
Osteoarthritis0.16 (0.07 to 0.25)0.57
Chronic headache0.15 (0.07 to 0.24)0.42

Read the authors' own conclusion rather than anyone's summary of it:

✦ Vickers et al 2012, verbatim"Significant differences between true and sham acupuncture indicate that acupuncture is more than a placebo. However, these differences are relatively modest, suggesting that factors in addition to the specific effects of needling are important contributors."

Both halves of that sentence are load-bearing. The confidence intervals for all three sham comparisons exclude zero, so the effect is statistically real. And an SMD of 0.15 to 0.23 is small - conventionally, 0.2 is the boundary of a "small" effect. The honest reading is: something is happening beyond placebo, and it is not large.

LiveThe 2018 update, and what happens after a year2 min▶

Vickers 2018 (The Journal of Pain 19(5):455-474) updated the same dataset to 39 trials and 20,827 patients. The headline figures held: roughly 0.5 standard deviations better than no acupuncture, roughly 0.2 standard deviations better than sham.

It added one number worth carrying: the effect decayed by about 15% at one year. That is a genuinely favourable finding for durability - most of the benefit was still measurable twelve months on - and it is also a reminder that these are chronic conditions where "still 85% of a small effect" is the realistic frame.

Use the 2012 paper for the per-condition numbers. A table in the 2018 update was internally inconsistent, so the 2012 figures are the ones to quote.

LiveCochrane on headache: the most careful reading available3 min▶

Migraine prophylaxis (Linde et al, Cochrane 2016): 22 trials, 4,985 participants. Against sham, SMD -0.18 (95% CI -0.28 to -0.08), rated moderate quality. Responder rates 50% versus 41%, giving a number needed to treat for an additional beneficial outcome of 11. The review's own verdict: an effect over sham exists, "but this effect is small."

Tension-type headache (Linde et al, Cochrane 2016): 51 versus 40 responders per 100 compared with sham; about 2 fewer headache days per month; "little or no difference in headache intensity"; GRADE moderate.

Notice what an NNTB of 11 means in a clinic rather than a table: for every eleven people given acupuncture rather than sham for migraine prevention, one additional person responds. That is a real effect that is also a modest one. Both descriptions are accurate, and which one a headline chooses tells you about the headline, not the evidence.

Real world

The NCCIH summary, October 2022. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health states plainly that "How acupuncture works is not fully understood," notes that it may help with back and neck pain, knee osteoarthritis and postoperative pain, and puts the two-gap finding in one sentence: "the difference between acupuncture and no treatment was greater than the difference between acupuncture and sham acupuncture." That is a federal health agency describing the same picture the diagram above draws.

LiveThe sham problem - an unresolved design problem, not ammunition3 min▶

Everything above rests on the sham comparison, so the sham comparison deserves scrutiny. A placebo control has to satisfy two requirements at once: it must be inert (no active effect of its own) and it must be indistinguishable from the real thing (so nobody can tell which arm they are in). Sugar pills manage both easily. Sham acupuncture reliably fails one or the other.

  • Minimal needling at non-points is indistinguishable, but it involves actually inserting needles into skin, which "may cause physiological effects." If your placebo is not inert, a null true-versus-sham result is ambiguous - it may mean the treatment does nothing, or it may mean the control was partly a treatment.
  • Non-penetrating devices (retractable needles, blunt tips) fix the inertness problem. But they introduce another: "it is impossible to blind the study investigators," and unblinded practitioners are a known route to bias.

Here is the part that matters. Both camps invoke this problem, and this course refuses to hand it to either. Proponents say it means the small sham gaps understate the real effect. Sceptics say it means the trials cannot cleanly demonstrate anything specific to needling. Both inferences are available from the same methodological fact, which is precisely what makes it unresolved rather than decisive.

The defensible position The sham-control problem is a genuine, acknowledged, unsolved design problem in acupuncture research. It is a reason to hold your conclusions loosely in both directions. It is not evidence for a conclusion, and anyone who deploys it as a trump card for their side is using a limitation as an argument.
Part 2 · covers herbal evidence quality and one success

Herbs: evidence quality and the one great success 8 min live

For acupuncture the problem is that the effects are small. For Chinese herbal medicine the problem is different and, in some ways, worse: there are thousands of trials, and most of them are not good enough to tell you anything. Then there is one enormous exception, and getting it correctly scoped is the most useful thing in this section.

LiveWhat the systematic reviews found when they looked3 min▶

Research says. The Cochrane overview (Hu et al, PLOS ONE 2011) examined 58 Cochrane reviews of Chinese herbal medicines. Of those, only 1 reached a positive conclusion. 27 (53%) concluded there "might be benefit" but were limited by study quality. 23 (45%) were inconclusive.

The reason sits in the underlying trials. Across 671 primary studies, only 16% had adequate sequence generation and only 7% had adequate allocation concealment - the two basic safeguards that make a randomised trial randomised.

A later AMSTAR-2 survey of the review literature itself found 96.6% (143 of 148) of Chinese herbal medicine systematic reviews to be of critically low methodological quality. Exactly one was rated high.

NCCIH's summary is the fair one-liner: studies "have had mixed results," and "Because many studies have been of poor quality, no firm conclusions can be made about their effectiveness."

LiveThe publication bias finding - and how to date it properly2 min▶

Vickers, Goyal, Harland & Rees, Controlled Clinical Trials 1998;19:159-166, examined a Medline sample of trials published 1966 to 1995 and found something startling: every acupuncture trial from China, Japan, Hong Kong and Taiwan was positive. Across all interventions, the positive-result rates were China 99%, Russia/USSR 97%, England 75%. The paper's blunt line: "No trial published in China or Russia/USSR found a test treatment to be ineffective."

Date it every time you cite it This is a 1998 analysis of pre-1996 literature, and the 100% figure is the acupuncture subset only, not all trials. Quoting it as a statement about research today is exactly the kind of dateless citation this course spent five sessions teaching you to catch. It remains important - it is a large part of why reviewers discount country-of-origin-skewed evidence bases - but it describes a specific literature at a specific time.
LiveArtemisinin - the honest positive, correctly scoped4 min▶

The strongest thing anyone can truthfully say for the classical Chinese pharmacopoeia is this story, and it is genuinely magnificent. It is also routinely told wrong.

  • The 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was divided. One half went jointly to William C. Campbell and 大村智 Satoshi Ōmura for avermectin, a roundworm therapy with nothing to do with Chinese medicine. The other half went to 屠呦呦 Tu Youyou alone, for artemisinin. It was not a three-way prize for traditional medicine.
  • Project 523 was launched on 23 May 1967 - the date is where the name comes from - as a military anti-malarial programme. Around 500 scientists across roughly 60 institutions took part. Tu was made a group head in 1969, aged 39.
  • The classical lead: 葛洪 Ge Hong's 《肘后备急方》, around 340 CE. The entry for 青蒿 qinghao specifies cold soaking, not hot decoction - take water, soak, wring out, drink the juice.
  • That processing detail is what mattered. It told Tu that heated extraction was destroying the compound, which led to low-temperature ether extraction. Neutral ether extract № 191 achieved 100% inhibition of Plasmodium berghei in mice in October 1971. Pure artemisinin was isolated in 1972.
★ The honest lesson, stated explicitly The classical text supplied two things: a screening hypothesis (look at this plant) and a processing hint (do not heat it). Everything after that was conventional pharmacology - extraction, animal models, isolation, structure determination, clinical trials, industrial synthesis. What the story validates is ethnobotany as a search heuristic: old pharmacopoeias are a good place to look for candidate molecules. It does not validate qi, meridians, five-phase correspondence, or pattern differentiation. None of those concepts appear anywhere in the causal chain.

One coda that makes the point sharper. The WHO discourages the use of Artemisia annua tea as a malaria treatment, because sub-therapeutic dosing drives resistance. The plant in its traditional preparation is not the medicine. The isolated, dosed, quality-controlled compound is.

Part 3 · covers documented harms and their sources

Safety: the part the packaging skips 9 min live

"Natural" is not a safety claim, and "traditional" is not a quality-control system. This section is not a scare list - every item is a documented, published, regulator-acknowledged event, and each one is here because it teaches a different failure mode: substitution, contamination, and interaction.

LiveAristolochic acid: the Belgian epidemic, told as the story it is4 min▶

Research says. Vanherweghem et al, The Lancet, 13 February 1993: nine women, all under 50, presenting in renal failure. All nine had attended one slimming clinic in Brussels.

The mechanism was not toxicity discovered late. It was botanical substitution. The clinic's formula changed in May 1990: Stephania tetrandra (汉防己) was replaced by Aristolochia fangchi (广防己). Why? Because in Chinese both plants are called 防己 fangji. Two species, two genera, one common name, and a supply chain that used the common name.

  • 100+ patients were ultimately affected; roughly 70 or more needed dialysis or a transplant.
  • Cancer followed. Nortier et al, NEJM 2000;342:1686-92: of 39 patients who underwent prophylactic nephroureterectomy, 18 had urothelial carcinoma - a prevalence of 46% (95% CI 29 to 62%). There was a dose-response relationship: 8 of 24 below 200 g cumulative dose versus 10 of 15 above it.

This is not an argument that Chinese herbs are dangerous. It is an argument about identification, sourcing and naming - the unglamorous infrastructure that pharmaceutical regulation exists to provide and that a common-name supply chain does not.

LiveIARC in two stages - get this exactly right2 min▶

The International Agency for Research on Cancer classified this in two separate steps, and the shorthand version people repeat collapses them:

VolumeYearWhat was classifiedGroup
Vol 822002Herbal remedies containing Aristolochia speciesGroup 1 (carcinogenic to humans)
Vol 822002Naturally occurring aristolochic acid mixturesGroup 2A (probably carcinogenic)
Vol 100A2012Aristolochic acid itself - upgradedGroup 1: "Aristolochic acid is carcinogenic to humans"

So "IARC classified aristolochic acid as Group 1 in 2002" is wrong. In 2002 the plants got Group 1 and the acid mixtures got 2A; the acid itself was upgraded in 2012. This is a small correction, and making it correctly is the same skill as the Sun Simiao correction in Session 5.

LiveRegulation, and the enforcement gap2 min▶

Regulators moved, and reasonably fast for the era:

  • United Kingdom, 1999: Aristolochia prohibited in herbal medicines, in force 28 July 1999. The MHRA describes the constituents as "nephrotoxic, carcinogenic, and mutagenic - even at very small doses."
  • United States: FDA Consumer Advisory of 11 April 2001, and Import Alert 54-10 to stop products at the border.

Then the part worth remembering. In 2003 the NEJM published a letter under the title "Aristolochic Acid, an Herbal Carcinogen, Sold on the Web after FDA Alert." A Group 1 carcinogen with an advisory, an import alert and a documented epidemic behind it was still purchasable online. Regulation of supplements is not the same thing as regulation of drugs, and the gap between an advisory and a market is where consumers actually live.

LiveContamination: what was actually in the bottles2 min▶

Ko RJ, "Adulterants in Asian Patent Medicines," NEJM 1998;339:847. The California Department of Health Services analysed 260 Asian patent medicines collected from retail outlets:

  • At least 83 (32%) contained undeclared pharmaceuticals or heavy metals.
  • 24 contained lead, median 30 ppm.
  • 36 contained arsenic, median 180 ppm.
  • 35 contained mercury, median 329 ppm.
  • 17 others contained undeclared conventional drugs.

NCCIH's current wording covers the same ground: Chinese herbal products have been found contaminated with "undeclared plant or animal material; drugs...; heavy metals (such as arsenic, lead, and cadmium); pesticides or...sulfites... or incorrect herbs, some of which have caused organ damage."

Nothing in this is about whether the traditional pharmacopoeia is any good. It is about what is in the bottle, which is a separate and prior question.

Self-studyHerb-drug interactions - the mechanism, with an honestly labelled example2 min read▶

The best-documented herb-drug interaction case is St John's wort, and it must be labelled accurately: this is a Western herb, not a Chinese one. It is here because it demonstrates the mechanism more clearly than any other single case.

  • FDA issued a Public Health Advisory on 10 February 2000, after an NIH study in 8 volunteers found St John's wort cut the AUC of the HIV protease inhibitor indinavir by a mean of 57%.
  • The route is CYP3A4 induction - the herb speeds up the liver enzyme system that clears a large fraction of prescription drugs. Faster clearance, lower blood levels, treatment failure.
  • NCCIH documents interactions with cyclosporine, oral contraceptives, warfarin and digoxin, and its implication in acute organ rejection in transplant patients.

The general lesson transfers: a plant preparation is a mixture of pharmacologically active molecules, and active molecules interact with other active molecules. "It is only a herb" is not a pharmacological category. Tell your doctor what you are taking - the one piece of practical advice this course will give, and it is a disclosure recommendation, not a treatment recommendation.

Part 4 · covers the legitimacy argument

ICD-11 and the argument about legitimacy 6 min live

In 2019 the World Health Organization did something that produced editorials in both Nature and Scientific American within ten weeks of each other. It is a good argument to end the evidence section on, because the disagreement is real, the participants are all serious, and this course does not get to adjudicate it.

LiveWhat was actually adopted2 min▶

The eleventh revision of the International Classification of Diseases was adopted at WHA72 in May 2019 and came into force on 1 January 2022. It includes Chapter 26, "Supplementary Chapter Traditional Medicine Conditions - Module 1" (TM1).

  • Codes SA00 to SJ1Z: roughly 150 disorders and roughly 196 patterns.
  • It harmonises Chinese, Japanese and Korean traditional-medicine classifications, which previously could not be compared with one another at all.
  • It is for optional dual coding, recorded alongside - never instead of - a conventional ICD-11 diagnosis.

WHO's own framing came from spokesperson Gregory Hartl, and it is worth quoting exactly because both sides of the argument cite it:

✦ WHO, verbatim"Inclusion in the ICD is not a recommendation of a treatment's efficacy; inclusion acknowledges that a disease or symptom or condition exists and that it should be measured."
LiveThe three positions, presented without a verdict3 min▶
PositionSourceThe argument
Criticism 1Scientific American editorial, 1 April 2019ICD inclusion "recklessly equates them with medicines that have undergone clinical trials." And: "Data supporting the effectiveness of most traditional remedies are scant, at best."
Criticism 2Nature editorial, 5 June 2019The breadth of the chapter risks legitimising the underlying philosophy, and is likely to "fuel the expanding sales of largely unproven treatments."
Counter-positionProponents of the chapterStandardised coding is a prerequisite for generating comparable safety and effectiveness data. You cannot study at scale what nobody records in a common vocabulary.

All three arguments are coherent, and they are partly about different things. The critics are arguing about signal - what a WHO code communicates to a patient, an insurer or a marketer. The proponents are arguing about measurement - what you need in place before evidence can accumulate. Those can both be right at once, which is exactly why the disagreement has not resolved.

The one factual guard-rail Whatever you conclude, the TM1 chapter is supplementary and optional, and it codes alongside a conventional diagnosis rather than replacing one. Claims that WHO "endorsed traditional Chinese medicine as effective" are not supported by what was adopted, and Hartl's sentence above was issued precisely to say so.
Part 5 · covers what to read next

How to keep reading 5 min live

You have read a few dozen passages in six weeks. If any of it caught, here is how to go further without falling into the wellness-market translation swamp - plus the two facts about the Neijing's standing today that are worth ending a course on.

On Veith, two honest notes. Later scholars have judged the work harshly, and the title itself mistranslates: 内经 is "inner canon," not "internal medicine" in the modern clinical sense. That mistranslation, from Session 1, has been shaping English-language expectations of this book since 1949.

And the negative recommendation, which matters as much: avoid uncited wellness-market translations entirely. If a version does not tell you which Chinese edition it worked from and does not carry textual notes, it cannot tell you the difference between the Han layers, Wang Bing's red ink, and a translator's guess. The primary text is free in Chinese at ctext.org.

LiveWhere the Neijing sits in the canon - two lists, both in use2 min▶

You will meet the phrase 中医四大经典, "the four great classics of Chinese medicine." There are two competing lists, and both are current:

  • List A (traditional): 《黄帝内经》 · 《难经》 · 《伤寒杂病论》 · 《神农本草经》
  • List B (modern curricular, used by the national 中医经典能力等级考试): 《黄帝内经》 · 《伤寒论》 · 《金匮要略》 · 温病学

They differ for a specific and unexciting reason: 张仲景's 《伤寒杂病论》 was later split into two books, 《伤寒论》 and 《金匮要略》. List A counts one, List B counts two, and the remaining slots shuffle accordingly.

In every version, the Neijing is number one. That is the claim that is safe to make, and it is enough.

LiveThe fact to end the course on1.5 min▶

Here is the verified fact that makes this text different from every other ancient medical classic you could have read.

★ A 2,000-year-old text is still a graded, examinable requirement for becoming a licensed doctor in China today. 内经选读 - "selected readings from the Neijing" - is a 必修课, a required course, for the TCM major. And the 2020 revision of the 医师资格考试大纲（中医）, the national licensing examination syllabus, broke out 中医经典 as a standalone examinable module. A dedicated 全国中医经典能力等级考试, with three levels, launched the same year.

Sit with what that means. The Hippocratic Corpus is read by historians. The Suwen is read by historians and sat as an exam by people who will hold a medical licence. Whatever you conclude about the evidence in Parts 1 to 3, this text is not a museum object - it is load-bearing infrastructure in a health system serving over a billion people. That is a reason to have read it carefully, and it is exactly why reading it carefully rather than credulously matters.

Read & apply · 🧩 final map assembly

Finish your Neijing map ✦ 12 min · the running project ends here

Six sessions of notes become one page. Everything in it is either a primary-text claim with a chapter number, a flagged seam, or a named study with exact numbers - and the last section is three lines that nobody can write for you.

Assemble what you have. The book facts, the yin-yang sort, the five-phase grid with its two seams, the zang-fu offices with the two translation warnings, and the seasonal regimens with the Sun Simiao correction. If any of them is missing a chapter number, go and get it - that is the difference between a map and a mood board.

Add an evidence block from today: the two acupuncture gaps, the herbal-evidence quality figures, artemisinin scoped correctly, and the three safety failure modes. Numbers exact, studies named.

Add the seam list in your seam colour - every place where the primary text and the received version differ. You now have five or six of them, and they are the most valuable thing on the page.

Then write the last section yourself. Three lines, no more, in your own words: what you think of the text as literature and history, what you think of the tradition as a living practice, and what you think of the evidence as it currently stands. Three separate judgements. Notice if you want to give the same answer three times - that is usually a sign one of them is doing the others' work.

Date it and keep it. Revisit it in a year. The evidence section will need updating; the text sections probably will not.

After the last session ◐ ongoing

  • Keep the journal through a full season change. Six weeks of entries let you compare against one chapter-2 paragraph. A full turn of the season lets you compare against two, and the comparison only means anything once you have watched the text's own gradient arrive on its own.
  • Read one Unschuld chapter properly. Not skimmed - properly, with the notes. Suwen ch.2 is a good candidate since you now know it well enough to see what a real translation adds over the renderings on this page.
  • Practise the correction habit. Every time you meet "the Neijing says," check it. You now know the two most common failures: a Tang-dynasty line credited to a Han text, and a four-way seasonal instruction flattened into one.
  • And the standing rail, one final time. If a health question in your life is live, this course does not answer it, and neither does the Neijing. NCCIH: "Don't use TCM to replace or delay seeking conventional care."

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Vickers et al 2012 found that acupuncture...

The authors' own words: "acupuncture is more than a placebo. However, these differences are relatively modest." Two gaps, two different sizes - and the space between them is where the argument actually lives.

2 · The artemisinin story validates...

Ge Hong's c.340 CE entry gave Tu Youyou a candidate plant and a processing hint ("do not heat it"). Everything after was conventional pharmacology. WHO in fact discourages A. annua tea, because sub-therapeutic dosing drives resistance.

3 · IARC's classification of aristolochic acid happened...

In 2002 the plants were Group 1 while the naturally occurring acid mixtures were Group 2A. The acid itself was upgraded in 2012: "Aristolochic acid is carcinogenic to humans." Getting the two stages right is the same skill as the Sun Simiao correction.
